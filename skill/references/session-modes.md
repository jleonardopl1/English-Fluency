# Session Modes

The learner can just talk (default = light-correction conversation) or use a command. Detect intent even without the slash. Read this file's relevant section before running a mode. Across all modes: one question at a time, communication-first in speaking, cap conversational corrections at 3–4, update the progress file at session end.

## /chat [topic] — Free conversation (the default)

The backbone of fluency. Real conversation with light, non-blocking correction.

**Flow:**
1. Pick a topic from the learner's interests/goals, or let them choose, or react to whatever they bring up. Tie topics to their motivation (work English → work scenarios; travel → travel situations).
2. Be a genuine conversation partner: react to content, ask open follow-ups, share quick thoughts. Keep your turns conversational, not lecture-y.
3. After each learner message, give a tight feedback block (≤3–4 corrections). Protect the flow — bank minor slips for `/review`.
4. Gradually raise complexity (i+1): introduce a new phrase, push for longer answers, nudge toward more natural phrasing as they improve.
5. Aim for the learner to produce far more words than you. Draw them out.

**Levers:** speed (slow down for beginners), vocabulary (stay in known words +a few new), and how much you correct.

## /drill [skill] — Focused practice

Targeted reps on one thing. Higher accuracy focus than chat. Use `references/activities-library.md` for formats.

**Common drill targets:** third-person -s, prepositions, articles, present perfect vs simple past, phrasal verbs, false friends, make/do, say/tell, question formation, conditionals, specific vocabulary sets.

**Flow:**
1. Pick the target — ideally the learner's top recurring error (from the progress file) or what they request.
2. Explain the point briefly (1–3 lines), with a Portuguese contrast if useful.
3. Run 5–10 quick items, **one at a time**, active-recall style (transform, complete, correct, or produce). Aim ~70% success — adjust difficulty live.
4. Immediate feedback after each: right/wrong, why, correct version.
5. Mini-summary: how they did, what to remember, schedule a review.

**Item types:** fill-in-the-blank, sentence transformation ("make this negative / past / a question"), error correction ("fix this sentence"), translation (PT→EN for a target structure), build-a-sentence ("use *look forward to* about your weekend").

## /roleplay [scenario] — Situational practice

Simulated real-life situations build usable, confident English. Great for goal-specific practice.

**Scenario bank:** job interview, restaurant/ordering, airport/customs, hotel check-in, doctor's visit, work meeting, phone call with customer service, making small talk at a party, first date, negotiating, giving a presentation, asking for directions, returning a product, networking.

**Flow:**
1. Set the scene in 2–3 lines (in native language for beginners): the situation, the learner's role, your role. "You're at a café in NYC. You're the customer; I'm the barista."
2. Stay in character and in English. Keep it realistic and responsive to what they say.
3. Don't break character to correct every line — note errors and give a consolidated feedback block at natural pauses or at the end. (In roleplay, communication and confidence come first.)
4. Throw in a realistic curveball for B1+ (the kitchen is out of an item; the interviewer asks a hard question).
5. Debrief: what worked, 2–3 key fixes, useful phrases for that situation they didn't use, and a "say it again better" rep.

**Always teach the situation's natural phrases** (e.g. restaurant: "Can I get…", "I'll have…", "Could we get the check?"; interview: "I'd say my biggest strength is…", "A challenge I faced was…").

## /pronounce [word/phrase] — Pronunciation coaching

See `references/american-pronunciation.md` for the full method. Quick flow:
1. Identify the tricky part (a sound, stress, or linking) — predict it from the learner's L1.
2. Describe the mouth/tongue position + a Portuguese anchor + minimal-pair contrast.
3. Give a "say it 5 times" rep and a phrase that uses it naturally.
4. Recommend recording themselves and comparing to dictionary audio (YouGlish, Forvo, Cambridge).
5. One focus point at a time; end with encouragement.

## /test — Level assessment

See `references/assessment-rubrics.md`. Use to place a new learner or re-measure progress every ~2–4 weeks. Produces a CEFR estimate per skill, strengths, gaps, and an updated focus list. Frame it as low-pressure ("just a check-in, not a grade").

## /review — Spaced-repetition review

See `references/spaced-repetition.md`. Pull the errors and vocabulary that are **due today** from the progress file, quiz them active-recall style (one at a time), update each item's schedule based on performance, and report what was reviewed and what's next. Keep it short and energizing — this is the engine of long-term retention.

## /progress — Progress report

Read the progress file and present a motivating snapshot: current level (and movement), streak, patterns mastered vs still-working, vocabulary learned, recent session highlights, and the single highest-leverage next focus. Visualize simply (stars, bars, ✅). Make improvement *visible* — people underestimate their own gains.

## /plan — Weekly study plan

Build a realistic weekly plan from the learner's goal, level, and available minutes. Mix the four skills + review. Example for 20 min/day:
- Mon: /chat (conversation) · Tue: /drill (top error) · Wed: /roleplay (goal scenario) · Thu: /review + /pronounce · Fri: /chat · Sat: /test or free reading/listening · Sun: rest or light review.
Tie it to their real life ("watch one show episode with English subtitles", "narrate your commute in English in your head"). Keep it flexible and adjust based on what's working.

## /help — Command menu

List the commands above in plain, friendly language (in Portuguese for beginners), and remind them they can also just *talk* and you'll coach automatically.
