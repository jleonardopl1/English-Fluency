# American Pronunciation Coaching (for Brazilian speakers)

Sounding native is mostly about a handful of sounds, **word stress**, **vowel reduction (schwa)**, and **connected speech**. Brazilians can reach near-native pronunciation by fixing the specific issues below. Coach these during `/pronounce` and whenever a learner asks how to say something — and proactively when you can tell from spelling/word choice that a word will trip them up.

You can't play audio, so coach with: (1) clear descriptions of mouth/tongue position, (2) Portuguese-anchored approximations, (3) minimal pairs to contrast, (4) "say it 5 times" reps, and (5) recommending they record themselves and compare to a dictionary's audio (e.g. dictionary.cambridge.org, Forvo, YouGlish).

## 1. The TH sounds — /θ/ (think) and /ð/ (this)

Portuguese has no TH. Brazilians replace it with **t/f/s** ("think" → "tink/fink/sink") or **d/z** ("this" → "dis/zis").

**How:** Put the tip of the tongue lightly between (or just behind) the teeth and push air through. Voiceless /θ/ = no vocal cord buzz (think, three, mouth, both, math). Voiced /ð/ = with buzz (this, that, the, mother, breathe).

**Minimal pairs:** think/sink · thin/fin · three/free · thank/sank · they/day · then/den · breathe/breeze.

Tip: exaggerate the tongue-between-teeth at first; it'll feel weird and that's correct.

## 2. The vowels Brazilians merge

English has more vowel distinctions than Portuguese. The big ones:

- **/iː/ vs /ɪ/** — "sheep" vs "ship". /iː/ = long, smiling, tense. /ɪ/ = short, relaxed, lower. Pairs: sheep/ship, beach/* (careful!), feel/fill, seat/sit, leave/live, heat/hit.
- **/æ/ vs /ɛ/** — "bad" vs "bed". /æ/ = open mouth, almost "é" but wider/flatter (cat, man, bad, happy). /ɛ/ = like PT "é" (bed, men, said). Pairs: bad/bed, man/men, sat/set, bat/bet.
- **/ʌ/** — "cup, but, love, money". A short central vowel, roughly between PT "a" and "ó", relaxed. Not the PT "u"! "but" is not "butt-oo".
- **/ɑː/ vs /ʌ/ vs /æ/** — "father" /ɑː/ (open, back) vs "cup" /ʌ/ vs "cat" /æ/. Pairs: cot/cut, lock/luck, body/buddy.
- **/uː/ vs /ʊ/** — "pool" vs "pull"; "fool" vs "full".
- **/ɔː/** — "thought, caught, law" (varies by US region; many Americans merge with /ɑː/).

Coach with the keyword + minimal pair, not the IPA symbol alone.

## 3. Final consonants — kill the extra vowel (epenthesis)

**The #1 accent giveaway for Brazilians.** Portuguese rarely ends words in consonants, so Brazilians add a vowel: "dog" → "dog-ee", "Facebook" → "Facebook-ee", "hot dog" → "hot dog-ee", "big" → "big-ee", "club" → "club-ee".

**Fix:** Stop the sound cleanly. The word ends ON the consonant. Practice: say "dog" and *cut it off* — tongue stops, no "ee".

Especially for final **-p, -b, -t, -d, -k, -g, -f, -v, -l, -m, -n, -s**. Drill: dog, web, hot, red, book, big, life, love, ball, time, sun, bus.

**Also -ED endings** (past tense). Three pronunciations, never "ed-ee":
- /t/ after voiceless sounds: walked, helped, watched, kissed, looked → "walkt".
- /d/ after voiced sounds: played, lived, called, opened, loved → "playd".
- /ɪd/ only after t/d: wanted, needed, decided, started → "want-id".

**Final -S/-ES** (plurals, 3rd person): /s/ after voiceless (cats, books), /z/ after voiced (dogs, goes, lives), /ɪz/ after s/z/sh/ch (buses, watches).

## 4. The schwa /ə/ and vowel reduction

This is the **secret to American rhythm**. Unstressed syllables collapse to a tiny, lazy "uh" sound (schwa). Brazilians give every vowel its full value, which sounds "syllable-timed" and foreign.

Examples (CAPS = stressed, the rest reduces to schwa):
- banana → "buh-NAN-uh"
- about → "uh-BOUT"
- computer → "kuhm-PYOO-ter"
- the / a / to / of / for / and → in fast speech: "thuh, uh, tuh, uhv, fer, uhn"
- "I want to go" → "I wanna go"; "going to" → "gonna"

Teach: stress one syllable hard, swallow the rest. English is **stress-timed**, not syllable-timed like Portuguese.

## 5. Word stress (gets you understood)

Stressing the wrong syllable is a top cause of "they didn't understand me." Patterns to teach:

- Most 2-syllable **nouns**: stress 1st (TAble, COFfee, PROblem). Many 2-syllable **verbs**: stress 2nd (deCIDE, reLAX, beGIN).
- Noun/verb pairs shift stress: **RE**cord (noun) vs re**CORD** (verb); **PRE**sent vs pre**SENT**; **CON**flict vs con**FLICT**.
- -tion / -sion / -ic / -ity → stress the syllable **before** the suffix: informaTION → infor**MA**tion; eleCTRic → e**LEC**tric; possiBILity → possi**BIL**ity.
- Compound nouns: stress the 1st part (GREENhouse, BUS stop, BIRTHday).

Always mark stress when teaching a new multi-syllable word.

## 6. The American R, T-flap, and dark L

- **American R /ɹ/** — NOT the Portuguese "rr" (which sounds like English /h/). Curl the tongue tip up and back without touching the roof; lips slightly rounded. Practice: red, car, world, girl, four, right. Watch the start of words: "really" should not become "heally".
- **T-flap** — between vowels, American T becomes a quick "d"-like tap: water → "wadder", better → "bedder", city → "ciddy", little → "liddle", get it → "geddit". This is very American and easy for Brazilians (similar to the PT "r" in *caro*).
- **Dark L** — at the end of words/syllables, L is "dark" (back of tongue raised): ball, feel, milk, cold, people. Don't turn it into a "u" sound like in PT (*Brasiu*). "milk" not "miuk".

## 7. Consonant clusters

Portuguese doesn't start words with s+consonant, so Brazilians add "e": "estop, espeak, esport, eschool, estudy". 

**Fix:** Start directly on the S, no "eh" before it. Hiss the S, then the next sound: s-top, s-peak, s-port, s-chool, s-tudy. Also practice clusters: street, spring, splash, texts, asked, clothes.

---

## 8. Connected speech (the native rhythm)

Natives don't say words one by one — they link them. Teaching this makes the biggest leap toward "native."

- **Linking consonant→vowel:** "an apple" → "a-napple"; "turn it off" → "tur-ni-toff"; "pick it up" → "pi-ki-tup".
- **Reductions:** wanna (want to), gonna (going to), gotta (got to), gimme (give me), lemme (let me), kinda, sorta, "whatcha doin'", "I dunno".
- **"of" → "uh/ə":** "cup of coffee" → "cuppa coffee"; "lots of" → "lotsa".
- **"and" → "n":** "rock and roll" → "rock'n'roll".
- **"you" → "ya/cha":** "did you" → "didja"; "got you" → "gotcha"; "what do you" → "whaddaya".
- **"h" drops** in unstressed pronouns: "tell him" → "tell'im"; "give her" → "give'er".

Don't overload — teach 1–2 reductions per session, in real phrases.

---

## 9. Intonation

- **Statements & wh-questions** fall at the end (↓): "Where are you GOing↓."
- **Yes/no questions** rise (↑): "Are you READy↑?"
- **Lists** rise on each item, fall on the last: "I bought apples↑, oranges↑, and bananas↓."
- **Content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) get stress; **function words** (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns) stay light. This stress-and-reduce wave IS the American sound.

---

## 10. Drill formats (for `/pronounce`)

1. **Minimal pairs:** give a pair, ask which one a sentence uses, or have them produce both. ("Say 'ship' then 'sheep' — feel the difference?")
2. **Targeted reps:** "Say this 5 times, slow → fast: 'I'd rather have water.'" (targets TH? no — targets flap-T + schwa).
3. **Stress marking:** show the word with the stressed syllable in CAPS and a quick rhythm: "comFORtable → actually 3 syllables: KUMF-ter-bul."
4. **Shadowing homework:** point them to a short clip on YouGlish/YouTube and tell them to repeat right after the speaker, copying rhythm and melody.
5. **Record & compare:** have them record a sentence and compare to a dictionary's audio; report back what felt different.

Always end a pronunciation note with encouragement and ONE clear thing to focus on next.
